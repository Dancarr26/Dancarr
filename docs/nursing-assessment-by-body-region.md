# Comprehensive nursing assessment — by body region

A working reference for a community RN comprehensive assessment: what is assessed
in each region, the anatomy behind it, where to put your hands, and what a finding
means. Written to be handed to a design brief for an anatomical visual guide, so
each region ends with **For a visual guide, show** — the structures and landmarks a
diagram would need to carry for that region to be useful at the bedside.

This is general nursing-assessment knowledge, not a transcription of any one
employer's form.

---

## 1. Vital signs and general observation

**Structures** Skin surface and superficial arteries (temporal, radial, brachial,
dorsalis pedis, posterior tibial), heart, lungs, peripheral capillary beds.

**Surface landmarks**
- Radial pulse — lateral (thumb side) volar wrist, over the distal radius
- Brachial — medial antecubital fossa, just medial to the biceps tendon; the BP
  auscultation point
- Apex / point of maximal impulse — 5th intercostal space, mid-clavicular line
- Dorsalis pedis — dorsum of the foot, lateral to the extensor hallucis longus tendon
- Posterior tibial — behind and below the medial malleolus

**What you assess**
- Temperature — site used matters; tympanic, oral and axillary are not interchangeable
- Pulse — rate, rhythm (regular / irregular), character (thready, normal, bounding)
- Apical rate — counted at the apex on auscultation; compare with the radial rate.
  A difference between the two is a **pulse deficit**, seen in atrial fibrillation
  and ectopics, and it is the reason both are counted
- Heart sounds — S1 (closure of mitral and tricuspid, start of systole), S2 (closure
  of aortic and pulmonic, end of systole), plus any added sound or murmur
- Respiratory rate, rhythm, depth — counted without the person knowing, or the rate
  changes
- Blood pressure sitting and standing — a **postural drop** is a fall in systolic
  ≥20 mmHg or diastolic ≥10 mmHg within 3 minutes of standing. It is a falls risk
  and a medication review trigger
- Oxygen saturation — and note whether on room air or oxygen, and whether the
  reading is reliable (cold, poorly perfused or painted nails give false readings)
- Height, weight, BMI — and whether weight has changed since last seen

**Why it is done this way** Vital signs are the baseline everything else is read
against. A single set is nearly meaningless; the trend is the finding.

**For a visual guide, show** the pulse points named above on a whole-body figure,
the four cardiac auscultation areas (aortic, pulmonic, tricuspid, mitral/apex) on
the anterior chest, and the BP cuff position relative to the brachial artery.

## 2. Neurological — cognition, mood, speech

**Structures** Cerebral cortex (frontal for executive function and behaviour,
temporal for memory and receptive language, parietal for sensation and neglect),
Broca's area (expressive speech, dominant frontal), Wernicke's area (receptive
speech, dominant temporal), cranial nerves.

**What you assess**
- **Orientation** — person, place, time, date. Deterioration in orientation over
  days suggests delirium, over months suggests dementia
- **Cognition** — normal, impaired, or formally screened. Screening tools in common
  use: Mini-Cog, MMSE, MoCA, RUDAS (designed for culturally and linguistically
  diverse populations), KICA-Cog (validated for Aboriginal and Torres Strait
  Islander people). Record which tool and the score, because scores are not
  comparable between tools
- **Speech** — nil deficit, slurred (dysarthria — a motor problem with the muscles
  of articulation), word-finding difficulty (expressive/Broca's aphasia — knows
  what they mean, can't retrieve it), comprehension difficulty (receptive/Wernicke's
  aphasia — fluent but doesn't follow), mixed, or non-verbal
- **Mood and affect** — normal, depressed, or described: content, happy, calm,
  flat, labile, irritable, tearful, apathetic, sad, withdrawn, anxious, restless,
  angry, hostile. Affect is what you observe; mood is what they report
- **Decision-making capacity** — can they make a day-to-day decision, and what is
  the reasoning behind your judgement. Capacity is decision-specific and can
  fluctuate; record the reasoning, not just the verdict

**Why it matters** Acute change in cognition is the single most useful early sign
of infection, dehydration, hypoxia, pain, retention or medication effect in an
older adult. It is often the only sign.

**For a visual guide, show** a lateral brain diagram with the frontal, temporal,
parietal and occipital lobes labelled, Broca's and Wernicke's areas marked, and
their function annotated in plain words.

## 3. Eyes and vision

**Structures** Eyelids, conjunctiva (palpebral lining the lid, bulbar over the
sclera), sclera, cornea, iris, pupil, lens, and the six extraocular muscles.

**Cranial nerves** II optic (vision), III oculomotor (pupil constriction, most eye
movement, lid elevation), IV trochlear (superior oblique), VI abducens (lateral
rectus).

**What you assess**
- **Glasses, near or far sighted, cataract surgery** — history that changes what
  you expect to find. A replaced lens does not accommodate
- **Pupils** — size in millimetres each side, equal or unequal, round or irregular,
  reaction to light (brisk / sluggish / fixed) and accommodation (present /
  sluggish / absent). Together this is **PERLA**
- **Conjunctiva and inner eyelids** — redness, swelling, discharge, lesions, and
  where
- **Extraocular movement** — the six cardinal positions of gaze, each side

**The six positions and the muscle each tests**

| Direction of gaze | Muscle | Nerve |
|---|---|---|
| Up and out (temporal) | Superior rectus | III |
| Up and in (nasal) | Inferior oblique | III |
| Out (temporal) | Lateral rectus | VI |
| In (nasal) | Medial rectus | III |
| Down and out (temporal) | Inferior rectus | III |
| Down and in (nasal) | Superior oblique | IV |

The two obliques are the counter-intuitive pair: **superior oblique takes the eye
down and in, inferior oblique takes it up and in.**

**How** Hold the head still, ask them to follow your finger through an H pattern
about 30 cm away, and watch for lag, nystagmus or complaint of double vision. Test
each eye and compare — asymmetry is the finding.

**What matters** A newly unequal or unreactive pupil is a neurological emergency
until proven otherwise. A fixed dilated pupil with a drooping lid and the eye
turned down-and-out is a third-nerve palsy. Sudden painless vision loss, or a
red painful eye with visual change, both need same-day review.

**For a visual guide, show** an eye with the six muscles and their direction of pull,
labelled medial/lateral so left and right are unambiguous; a pupil size chart in
millimetres; and the conjunctival surfaces (palpebral and bulbar) named.

## 4. Ears and hearing

**Structures** Pinna, external auditory canal, tympanic membrane, middle ear
ossicles, cochlea, vestibular apparatus. **Cranial nerve VIII** vestibulocochlear.

**What you assess**
- **Hearing**, each side — many people have asymmetric loss and compensate
- **Hearing aids** — whether worn, whether working, whether they have batteries.
  An unworn aid in a drawer is a very common finding and a very common cause of
  apparent confusion
- **Ear canal** — clear or occluded with wax. Impacted cerumen is a reversible
  cause of hearing loss and worth checking before anything else is concluded
- **Tympanic membrane** — should be **pearly grey, translucent, intact**, with a
  visible cone of light. Record redness, bulging, retraction, perforation, fluid,
  or that it could not be visualised

**How** Straighten the canal by pulling the pinna up and back in an adult (down and
back in a young child). Brace the otoscope hand against the head so a sudden
movement doesn't drive the speculum in.

**What matters** Unaddressed hearing loss is strongly associated with social
withdrawal, low mood and accelerated cognitive decline — it is not a minor finding
in an older adult. Sudden unilateral loss needs urgent review.

**For a visual guide, show** a cross-section of the ear canal and middle ear, and a
normal tympanic membrane with the cone of light, pars tensa, umbo and handle of
malleus marked, so abnormal has something to be compared against.

## 5. Nose and sinuses

**Structures** External nose, nasal septum, turbinates, nasal mucosa, olfactory
epithelium, and the paranasal sinuses — frontal (above the orbit), maxillary (over
the cheek), ethmoid and sphenoid (deep, not palpable). **Cranial nerve I**
olfactory.

**What you assess**
- **Smell** — can they identify a familiar scent held under the nose. Loss of
  smell may be age-related, post-viral, or an early neurodegenerative sign
- **Patency** — occlude one nostril at a time and ask them to sniff
- **Symmetry** — of the external nose and the septum
- **Nasal flaring** — a sign of increased work of breathing, not a nose finding
- **Discharge** — clear, purulent, bloody, unilateral or bilateral. Persistent
  unilateral discharge in an adult warrants investigation
- **Epistaxis** — frequency, which side, and what they take (anticoagulants,
  antiplatelets, nasal steroids all matter here)
- **Sinus tenderness** — palpate and gently percuss the frontal sinuses (under the
  brow ridge, pressing upward) and the maxillary sinuses (over the cheekbones,
  pressing upward). Only these two are accessible

**For a visual guide, show** the paranasal sinuses on a frontal face view with the
frontal and maxillary marked as palpable and the ethmoid/sphenoid marked as not,
plus the palpation points and direction of pressure.

## 6. Mouth, throat and swallowing

**Structures** Lips, buccal mucosa, gums, teeth, tongue, hard and soft palate,
uvula, tonsils, oropharynx, epiglottis. **Cranial nerves** V trigeminal (chewing,
facial sensation), VII facial (taste anterior two-thirds of tongue), IX
glossopharyngeal and X vagus (gag, swallow, palate elevation), XII hypoglossal
(tongue movement).

**What you assess**
- **Teeth** — own, dentures, or both; broken, decayed; and whether dentures
  actually fit. Weight loss in an older adult is very often a dental problem
- **Gums, buccal mucosa, tongue** — should be pink and moist; record lesions,
  ulceration, white plaques (candida, common on inhaled steroids), coating
- **Lips** — moist, lesion-free; check the corners for angular cheilitis
- **Tongue** — appearance (pink and moist, dry, coated, fissured, red and beefy,
  ulcerated), protrudes midline without deviation, full movement. Deviation points
  toward the side of a hypoglossal lesion
- **Oropharynx** — soft palate rises symmetrically, uvula midline
- **Taste** — sweet versus salty discrimination
- **Gag reflex** — touch the posterior pharyngeal wall. Graded: 0 absent (always
  abnormal), 1+ slight, 2+ brisk and normal, 3+ very brisk, 4+ repeating/clonus
  (always abnormal). An absent gag does not by itself predict aspiration, and a
  present gag does not rule it out — it is one datum, not a swallow screen
- **Swallowing** — difficulty with food, with fluids, or both; coughing or a wet
  voice during or after eating or drinking; whether a modified diet or thickened
  fluids are already in place, and whether that is being followed

**What matters** Coughing on fluids, a wet gurgly voice after swallowing, food
pocketing in the cheek, prolonged mealtimes and recurrent chest infections together
point to aspiration risk and need a speech pathology referral.

**For a visual guide, show** an open-mouth view with buccal mucosa, gums, hard and
soft palate, uvula, tonsillar pillars and posterior pharyngeal wall labelled, and a
separate tongue view for deviation.

## 7. Neck

**Structures** Trachea, thyroid gland (isthmus over the 2nd–4th tracheal rings,
lobes either side), carotid arteries, internal jugular veins, cervical lymph node
chains, cervical spine.

**What you assess**
- **Trachea midline** — palpate in the suprasternal notch, a finger either side of
  the trachea, comparing the gap. Deviation is pushed away by a tension
  pneumothorax or large effusion, pulled toward by collapse or fibrosis
- **Range of movement** — full, reduced, painful, rigid. Neck rigidity with
  headache and fever is meningism
- **Lymph nodes** — palpate the chains systematically: pre- and post-auricular,
  occipital, tonsillar, submandibular, submental, superficial and deep cervical,
  posterior cervical, supraclavicular. Record site, size, consistency, mobility and
  tenderness. **A hard, fixed, painless supraclavicular node is a red flag**
- **Thyroid** — enlargement, symmetry, nodules; ask them to swallow and watch the
  gland rise
- **JVP** — with the patient at 45°, measure the vertical height of the venous
  pulsation above the sternal angle; more than 3–4 cm suggests raised right atrial
  pressure and fluid overload
- **Carotid bruit** — listen with the bell over each carotid while they hold their
  breath. **Do not palpate both carotids at once, and palpate gently** — carotid
  sinus massage can drop the heart rate

**For a visual guide, show** the cervical lymph node chains on a lateral neck view,
the thyroid outline against the tracheal rings, the carotid auscultation point, and
the sternal angle reference line for JVP.

## 8. Respiratory — chest and lungs

**Structures** Chest wall, ribs, intercostal muscles, accessory muscles
(sternocleidomastoid, scalenes, trapezius), diaphragm, pleura, and the lung lobes —
**right has three (upper, middle, lower), left has two (upper and lower)**, with the
lingula as part of the left upper lobe.

**Surface landmarks**
- Anterior: mid-sternal, mid-clavicular and anterior axillary lines; count ribs from
  the sternal angle (2nd rib)
- Posterior: vertebral, scapular and posterior axillary lines; C7 is the most
  prominent spinous process
- The right middle lobe is only reachable **anteriorly**, low on the right. The
  lower lobes occupy most of the **posterior** field — which is why a chest listened
  to only from the front misses most basal pathology

**What you assess**
- **Smoking** — current, past, pack-years
- **Respiratory effort** — at rest versus on activity; ask what they can do before
  becoming short of breath
- **Colour of lips and mucous membranes** — pink, cyanosed (central cyanosis in the
  lips and tongue means hypoxaemia), pale, jaundiced
- **Posture** — tripod position, leaning forward on the arms, indicates significant
  effort
- **Shape of chest** — normal, barrel (increased AP diameter, chronic air trapping),
  pectus excavatum or carinatum, kyphoscoliosis
- **Symmetry and expansion** — hands flat on the posterior chest, thumbs meeting at
  the spine, and watch the thumbs separate equally on inspiration
- **Accessory muscle use** — a sign of work, visible at the neck
- **Palpation of the thorax** — tenderness (suggests a musculoskeletal or rib cause
  rather than a lung one), crepitus, masses
- **Auscultation** — compare side to side at each level, never down one side then
  the other. Nine sites is the usual sequence: three anterior, six posterior
- **Wheeze** — musical, usually expiratory, from narrowed airways; note where
- **Crackles** — discontinuous; fine late-inspiratory basal crackles suggest fluid
  or fibrosis, coarse crackles that clear with coughing suggest secretions. Note
  where, and whether dry, moist or productive
- **Cough and sputum** — colour, amount, consistency, odour; and whether it is new
- **Clubbing** — loss of the nail-bed angle; suggests chronic hypoxia, bronchiectasis,
  or malignancy

**For a visual guide, show** anterior and posterior chest outlines with the lobes
shaded and named, L and R marked on both views, and the auscultation sequence
numbered — making clear that the right middle lobe is anterior-only and the lower
lobes are mostly posterior.

## 9. Abdomen — stomach and gastrointestinal

**Structures** By quadrant — right upper (liver, gallbladder, hepatic flexure),
left upper (stomach, spleen, splenic flexure, pancreas tail), right lower (caecum,
appendix, right ovary), left lower (sigmoid colon, left ovary); epigastrium,
umbilical and suprapubic regions centrally.

**Order matters: inspect, auscultate, percuss, palpate.** Palpating first changes
the bowel sounds you then hear.

**What you assess**
- **Inspection** — soft, distended, scars, hernia, stoma, bruising. Flank or
  periumbilical bruising is a late sign of retroperitoneal bleeding
- **Bowel sounds** — present and normal, hyperactive (obstruction early, gastro),
  hypoactive or absent (ileus, late obstruction, peritonitis). Listen in one place
  for long enough before calling them absent
- **Palpation** — light first across all quadrants, then deep, watching the face not
  the hand. Soft and non-tender, tender, guarding (involuntary muscle tensing),
  rigid. **Tender the painful quadrant last**
- **Site of tenderness** — record by quadrant
- **Masses** — site, size, mobility, pulsatility. A pulsatile expansile central mass
  is an abdominal aortic aneurysm until excluded
- **Reflux and heartburn** — frequency, night-time symptoms, what relieves it
- **History of ulcer or gastritis**, and current NSAID or anticoagulant use
- **Nausea, vomiting, epigastric pain**

**For a visual guide, show** the four quadrants and nine regions on an abdominal
outline, with the organ lying under each, plus the auscultation and palpation
sequence.

## 10. Bowel and stoma

**What you assess**
- **Usual bowel habit** — their normal, not a textbook normal
- **Continence of faeces**
- **Stool type** — Bristol Stool Chart, 1 (separate hard lumps) to 7 (watery, no
  solid pieces). Types 3 and 4 are the target
- **Aperients** — what, how often, and whether effective. Opioids, anticholinergics,
  iron and calcium channel blockers all constipate
- **Stoma**, where present — type (colostomy, ileostomy, urostomy, jejunostomy),
  **stoma colour** (should be pink to red and moist; pale, dusky or black indicates
  compromised blood supply and is urgent), peristomal skin, output type and volume,
  bag change due or completed, and any pain, bleeding, pus or bloating

**What matters** Overflow diarrhoea around a faecal impaction presents as
incontinence of liquid stool in someone who "has diarrhoea". New change in bowel
habit, blood in the stool, or unexplained weight loss needs investigation.

**For a visual guide, show** the colon with the common stoma sites marked against
the segment they come from, the Bristol chart, and a normal versus compromised
stoma colour comparison.

## 11. Continence and urinary

**What you assess**
- **Continence** — continent, or the pattern of incontinence: stress (on cough,
  laugh, lift), urge (sudden, can't defer), mixed, overflow, functional (can't get
  there in time)
- **Aids used** — type, size, how many per day, who funds them
- **Toilet access** — can they get there independently, and is the path safe at
  night. Toileting at night is a leading falls scenario
- **Nocturia** — how many times overnight
- **Catheter or other urinary drainage** — type, size, date last changed, drainage,
  and the plan for removal
- **Urinalysis** — with the caution that **asymptomatic bacteriuria is common in
  older adults and should not be treated on a dipstick alone**. Look for symptoms

**For a visual guide, show** the bladder, urethra and pelvic floor with the male and
female differences, and the continence types with their mechanism annotated.

## 12. Extremities and peripheral circulation

**Structures** Limbs, peripheral arteries and veins, nails, joints, and the
lymphatic drainage.

**What you assess**
- **Colour of limbs** — pallor, redness, mottling, cyanosis, and whether it changes
  with elevation or dependency
- **Temperature** — compare side to side with the back of your hand. A cool, pale,
  pulseless, painful limb is arterial until excluded
- **Capillary refill** — press the nail bed 5 seconds and release; under 2 seconds
  is normal, over 3 is delayed
- **Peripheral pulses** — radial and pedal, each side: present, weak, absent
- **Oedema** — location, and pitting depth graded: 1+ slight (2 mm), disappears
  rapidly; 2+ moderate (4 mm); 3+ deep (6 mm), may last over a minute, obvious
  swelling; 4+ very deep (8 mm), lasting two to five minutes. Bilateral ankle oedema
  is usually cardiac, venous or drug-related; **unilateral, warm, tender swelling
  suggests DVT**
- **Compression stockings or leg elevation** — in use, correctly fitted, and
  tolerated. Compression on an arterial leg causes harm — the ABPI matters
- **Varicose veins**
- **Nails** — colour (pinkish hue normal; blue, black, white, yellow, green all
  mean something different), shape, clubbing, and nail care (well kept, overgrown,
  thickened, fungal, podiatry in place)
- **Tremor, contractures, foot deformity**
- **Footwear** — appropriate, unsafe, slippers only, barefoot. This is a falls
  finding as much as a clothing one

**For a visual guide, show** the arterial tree of the leg with the palpation points,
the pitting oedema grades illustrated by depth, and a nail-change comparison plate
including clubbing in profile.

## 13. Skin

**Structures** Epidermis, dermis, subcutaneous tissue, hair follicles, sebaceous and
sweat glands, and the pressure-bearing bony prominences.

**Pressure points to inspect** Occiput, ears, scapulae, elbows, sacrum, coccyx,
ischial tuberosities, greater trochanters, knees, malleoli, heels, and between the
toes.

**What you assess**
- **Colour** — normal, pale, flushed, cyanosed, mottled, jaundiced. Assess in
  natural light; in darker skin tones look for changes in hue against surrounding
  skin, and rely more on **temperature, firmness and the person's report of pain**
  than on redness
- **Temperature, moisture and texture** — warm, cool, clammy, cold, dry, scaly,
  broken
- **Turgor** — unreliable in older adults, but a gross change still tells you something
- **Perfusion** — good, poor, cool
- **Sensation** — normal, reduced, absent, tingling, and where. Map it. Reduced
  protective sensation in the feet changes the whole foot-care plan
- **Lesions and wounds** — site, size, depth, edges, base, exudate, odour,
  surrounding skin. Photograph and chart separately
- **Foot care in diabetes** — inspect between the toes, check for callus, deformity,
  ulceration and pulses, and confirm who is cutting the nails

**What matters** Non-blanching erythema over a bony prominence is a stage 1 pressure
injury and the last point at which it is fully reversible. A skin tear, a pressure
injury and a moisture lesion are three different things with three different
management paths.

**For a visual guide, show** a whole-body figure front and back with pressure points
marked, a skin-layer cross-section with pressure injury stages 1–4 plus unstageable
and deep tissue injury, and the skin tear categories.

## 14. Musculoskeletal, mobility and falls

**Structures** Joints — cervical spine, shoulders, elbows, forearms, wrists, fingers
and thumbs, hips, knees, ankles and toes, and the spine with its cervical, thoracic
and lumbar curves. Muscles, tendons, ligaments, and the cerebellar and vestibular
systems that keep it all upright.

**What you assess**
- **Functional transfers** — mobilises unaided, turns over in bed, supine to sit,
  sit to stand, bed to chair. Watch it done rather than ask
- **Posture** — body erect, head upright, normal spinal curvature
- **Gait and balance** — coordinated, balanced and effortless; turning smooth.
  Turning is where instability shows first
- **Romberg's test** — stand feet together, eyes open then closed. Losing balance
  only with the eyes closed is a positive Romberg and points to a proprioceptive or
  vestibular problem rather than a cerebellar one
- **Joints, one by one** — crepitus, heat, redness, swelling, pain, deformity
- **Muscle strength**, 0–5: 0 no movement; 1 barely detectable contraction; 2 full
  range with gravity eliminated; 3 full range against gravity; 4 full range against
  gravity and some resistance but weak; 5 normal
- **Falls history** — any fall in the last three months, how many, where, what they
  were doing, whether they could get up, and whether anyone was told
- **Aids** — what they have, whether it is the right one, whether it is the right
  height, and whether they actually use it

**What matters** A fall is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Work backwards through
postural drop, medication (especially sedatives, antihypertensives, anticholinergics
and anything started recently), vision, footwear, continence, environment, pain and
cognition.

**For a visual guide, show** a skeleton with the assessed joints marked, the three
spinal curves labelled, and the transfer sequence illustrated step by step.

## 15. Pain

**Assess with a structure — OPQRSTU or similar**

- **Onset** — when it started, what they were doing, sudden or gradual
- **Provoking and relieving** — what makes it worse, what helps, what they have tried
- **Quality** — their words: burning, shooting, aching, cramping, stabbing. Burning,
  shooting or electric quality suggests **neuropathic** pain, which responds poorly
  to simple analgesia
- **Region and radiation** — where, and where it travels
- **Severity** — a consistent scale, and which scale. For someone with dementia use
  an observational tool (PAINAD, Abbey) and score behaviour, not self-report
- **Timing** — constant or intermittent, pattern through the day, what it stops them
  doing
- **Understanding** — what they believe is causing it and what they want done

Also record **acute versus chronic**, the presumed cause, and associated factors —
nausea, mood, appetite, sleep.

**What matters** Pain in an older adult with cognitive impairment is routinely
under-treated and often presents as agitation, resistance to care, withdrawal or a
sudden functional decline rather than a complaint of pain.

**For a visual guide, show** a front and back body outline for marking and radiation,
the pain quality descriptors grouped by likely mechanism (nociceptive versus
neuropathic), and a scale appropriate to cognitive level.

## Cross-cutting notes for a visual guide

1. **Left and right must be unambiguous on every figure.** Anterior and posterior
   views mirror each other, and an unlabelled diagram is the most common source of
   a side error.
2. **Show the landmark, not just the organ.** The usable information is where to
   put your hands and stethoscope, and what lies under that spot.
3. **Paired structures get both sides.** Eyes, ears, lungs, limbs and pulses are all
   assessed by comparison — asymmetry is the finding, so the diagram should invite
   a side-by-side.
4. **Grades and scales need a picture, not a number.** Pitting depth, pressure injury
   stages, Bristol types, clubbing angle and pupil size in millimetres are all far
   more reliable read off an image than recalled.
5. **Colour findings need darker skin tones represented**, or the guide will only
   work for some of the people being assessed.
